# Re-review what changed, then stop on materiality

The published evidence supports a clear split. **Running the same review again over the same unchanged scope has little value.** Re-reviewing **unchanged text because something it touches has changed** is necessary, and that is where the danger lies in a rule that forbids repeat review. The standards and inspection literature set the minimum re-review as **the changed areas plus their side effects**, checked by someone other than the author. A full re-review is triggered only by heavy rework (Fagan's 5%) or a change in the document's standing. The research gives two reasons a fix pass must itself be reviewed: fixes inject new defects at measured rates of 7–40% (all measured on code), and fixes in specifications cascade into unchanged text. On when to stop, the literature points to **severity**: the number of major defects drives the net gain of reinspection. Raw finding counts are a poor signal, and statistical estimates are unusable with one or two reviewers. With fresh LLM reviewers this matters more. Repeated runs re-find the same core and add unstable extras, critics find problems in work judged flawless, and so "a round with zero findings" is partly luck. The stop signal that survives is **no new finding the author rules material**, with the author deciding. Read the evidence with care. The inspection research is mostly from 1976–2004, about code or short requirements documents, and done on student or industrial teams. The LLM evidence is mostly 2024–2026 preprints, one of them a single-author case study and two of them vendor-run. No study examines a human-gated, single-author design-document review loop.

## A repeat over an unchanged scope adds little, but "unchanged" must mean the inputs did not change

Every strand of the literature finds diminishing returns from more reviewers or more passes of the same kind. Biffl and Gutjahr found the benefit of an additional inspector "diminished quickly with growing team size" ([dblp](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/metrics/BifflG01.html)). Kantorowitz's model has the fault-detection ratio rising toward a ceiling **below 1**, because reviewers "with insufficient domain knowledge may not be able to detect certain types of faults". Extra rounds by similar reviewers therefore flatten out ([Harel & Kantorowitz](https://csaws.cs.technion.ac.il/~kantor/publications/IterativeCodeReviews.pdf)). In practice, a second round is common, but it is used to verify fixes, not to hunt again. A 2003 survey reported that **35% of companies run a second code-review iteration, "only to make sure that faults found at the first iteration were corrected"** (Ciolkowski, Laitenberger and Biffl, as cited in [Harel & Kantorowitz](https://csaws.cs.technion.ac.il/~kantor/publications/IterativeCodeReviews.pdf)). No study measured a second identical pass over unchanged material as a separate condition. Biffl et al.'s reinspections followed rework, so the document had already changed ([dblp](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/icse/BifflFL01.html)).

For LLM reviewers, there is one direct measurement of rerunning a review over identical input. It is Snyk's VulnBench, run on Claude models over code. Findings that matched the reference set were highly stable: **84.8% appeared in all five of five runs**. "Extra" findings were not: **49.7% appeared in only one run and 13.7% in all five** ([arXiv 2606.15762](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15762)). A rerun over unchanged text therefore mostly re-finds what the first run found, plus a batch of extras that will mostly not recur. Whether those extras were real was never triaged. The rerun does have one honest use: **recurrence across independent runs is a cheap stability signal**. It is not a way to find new material defects.

Lifting the ceiling takes a different lens, not the same lens again. Biffl and Halling found that teams mixing reading techniques are considerably more effective than teams all using the best single technique, and that above a certain size, the right process mix matters more than another inspector ([index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/81834825)). N-fold inspection gets its gains because independent teams find largely different faults. A secondary report of Martin and Tsai says that two teams found 1.5 times as many faults as one, and no fault was found by all teams ([Harel & Kantorowitz](https://csaws.cs.technion.ac.il/~kantor/publications/IterativeCodeReviews.pdf)). The setting already has three lenses: section, whole document, and seam. That is the evidence-favoured way to spend extra review on the same text.

The danger in a no-repeat rule is real, but it comes from **change ripple**, not from repetition. Real specifications restate themselves heavily. Across **28 industrial specifications (8,667 pages), average clone coverage was 13.6%**, one was about two-thirds duplicated, and the tool repeatedly found copies that had drifted apart "from inconsistent modification" ([Juergens et al., ICSE 2010](https://itestra.com/media/publications/clone-detection-requirements.pdf)). The same authors warn that inspections which "analyze the different parts of a specification individually" are "prone to miss duplication" ([Juergens et al.](https://itestra.com/media/publications/clone-detection-requirements.pdf)). In the one LLM audit of a multi-file specification, **27% of 51 consistency defects spanned more than one file**. Fixes produced "cascading edits": "Fixing issue A in round N can create a new inconsistency B that was masked by A". One example: removing a config flag in round 5 made a changelog entry elsewhere newly wrong, and it was caught only in round 6 ([Calboreanu, arXiv 2605.12280](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12280)). So yes, a change elsewhere can make unchanged text wrong. A rule keyed on "this text has not changed" would skip exactly that text.

The resolution is to key the rule on whether **anything the scope depends on has changed**, not on whether its own bytes have. Even then, rerunning the *section* review is the wrong tool for ripple. A reviewer scoped to the section cannot, by construction, see a conflict whose other half lies outside its scope. Calboreanu states this explicitly for file-scoped review ([arXiv 2605.12280](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12280)). The instrument that catches ripple is an anchored check of the change against the text that depends on it, or a seam review. A rule that forbids repeating an identical section review is therefore defensible, provided some other stage is obliged to look at the unchanged text a change can break.

## After fixes land, the re-review scope is the change, its dependants, its restatements and its seams

The standards set a floor. IEEE 1028, quoted secondhand because the text is paywalled, requires that "at a minimum, a reinspection shall examine the software product areas changed to resolve anomalies identified in the last inspection, as well as side effects of those changes". It also makes rework verification the job of "the inspection leader or a designated member of the inspection team **(other than the author)**" ([IPOL post quoting IEEE 1028](https://tools.ipol.im/mailman/archive/discuss/2012-February/000815.html)). Fagan's original rule scales the scope with how much was changed. "If more than five percent of the material has been reworked, the team should reconvene and carry out a 100 percent reinspection". Below that, the moderator checks the rework or reconvenes the team at his discretion ([Fagan 1976](https://www.ida.liu.se/~TDDC90/labs/lab-papers/fagan76.pdf)). Industry processes classify the *kind* of change rather than its size. The W3C splits changes into editorial ones (no text change, or no functional effect on interpretation) and substantive ones, and rules that **any doubt about functional effect makes a change substantive** ([W3C Process](https://www.w3.org/policies/process/)). Gerrit carries an approval forward only when the change kind shows the meaning is unchanged; otherwise the vote is "outdated" ([Gerrit config-labels](https://gerrit-review.googlesource.com/Documentation/config-labels.html)). Safety standards set the re-verification scope by change-impact analysis along traceability links. That evidence is secondary, because the normative texts are paywalled ([Parasoft on DO-178C](https://parasoft.com/learning-center/do-178c/regression-testing)).

Two bodies of evidence say why applied fixes deserve their own check. First, fixes inject defects. Capers Jones's benchmark puts **about 7% of bug fixes containing a new bug**, rising to 25% or more in very complex code. His derivation is not published ([CERM Academy](https://insights.cermacademy.com/?p=342)). Yin et al. measured **14.8–24.4% of post-release OS fixes as incorrect** ([ESEC/FSE 2011](https://www.eecg.utoronto.ca/~yuan/papers/incorrect_fix_abstract.html)). Purushothaman and Perry found **nearly 40% of fault-fixing changes at Lucent led to further faults** ([IEEE TSE 2005](https://users.ece.utexas.edu/~perry/work/papers/DP-03-small.pdf)). Fagan listed "follow-up to reduce bad fixes" as a defining property of inspection ([Fagan 1976](https://www.ida.liu.se/~TDDC90/labs/lab-papers/fagan76.pdf)). All of these figures are for code; no bad-fix rate for prose was found. Second, people under-predict impact. In a four-year Ericsson study, developers judged individual impacted items well, but **the actual number of changed items was usually higher than predicted**, and developers did not know where they under-predicted ([Lindvall & Sandahl, JSS 1998](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/jss/LindvallS98.html)).

Bohner and Arnold's framework sets out the shape of a defensible scope. Start from the changed items, expand along dependency links to a candidate impact set, and judge the method by whether the actual impact falls inside it ([Arnold & Bohner, ICSM 1993](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/xyzhang/fall07/Papers/00366933.pdf)). Applied to this setting, the scope after fixes has four layers:

1. **The changed text itself**, checked against its intent.
2. **Its explicit dependants**: sections that cite it, or that use a name, value or rule it defines.
3. **Its implicit dependants**: places that restate the same fact without citing it. The citation graph misses these, which is why Juergens et al. recommend mechanical duplicate detection, at under one person-hour per document ([Juergens et al.](https://itestra.com/media/publications/clone-detection-requirements.pdf)).
4. **Every seam that shares a name or contract with the change.** Calboreanu's most consequential defect was a seam defect: one lane emitted `priority_score` while the next consumed `fix_priority`. It was found only in round 8, by a full-scope pass that compared the schemas field by field ([arXiv 2605.12280](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12280)).

This layering is a synthesis. No source prescribes it for design documents, and no study compares it head-to-head with re-running the whole-document review.

A whole-document pass belongs **at the end, before declaring done**. It should not be part of every fix cycle. Impact sets predicted from dependencies run short, so a final pass catches what the dependency trace missed. Fagan's 5% threshold is the only published number for when heavy rework justifies a full pass earlier. Note that it was set for team code inspections in 1976.

How the re-review is phrased matters as much as its scope. In ContraDoc, GPT-4 asked whether a document contradicts itself had **8% recall**. Told a contradiction existed, it located the evidence in its top five 70% of the time. Given one side and asked for the other, it **found the partner 77% of the time** ([ContraDoc, NAACL 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09182v2)). So a post-fix check should say "this passage changed from X to Y; find every place that states, assumes or depends on X". It should not ask "is this consistent?". More context is not automatically better. In one benchmark, all eight frontier models got **worse as raw context grew** from diff-only to full context, and a structured 2,000-token diff-with-summary beat a 2,500-token full dump ([SWE-PRBench, arXiv 2603.26130](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.26130)). That is a single-author preprint on code, but it points the same way: widen the scope to the specific dependants, and do not dump everything in.

## Severity, not counts or statistics, should decide whether another round runs

The table sets out the candidate decision signals against the evidence for each.

| Signal | What the evidence says | Fitness here |
|---|---|---|
| Significance of findings | Reinspection net gain is driven chiefly by **estimated benefit and the number of major defects** ([Biffl et al., ICSE 2001](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/icse/BifflFL01.html)); Gilb & Graham's exit gates count *major* defects per page (secondary) | Best supported |
| Amount of text the fixes changed | Fagan: >5% rework means 100% reinspection ([Fagan 1976](https://www.ida.liu.se/~TDDC90/labs/lab-papers/fagan76.pdf)); W3C/Gerrit classify by meaning, not size ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/policies/process/)) | Good trigger for scope; *kind* of change (meaning-altering, contract-touching) matters more than volume |
| Count of findings | Count thresholds invite inspectors to find "a passing number of defects" ([Briand et al., ISERN-97-22](https://ehealthinformation.ca/web/default/files/wp-files/isern-97-22.pdf)); LLM extras are mostly unstable ([Snyk](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15762)); critics find problems in flawless work ([CriticGPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00215v1)) | Poor |
| Statistical estimate | Capture–recapture "ought not be used with less than 4 inspectors" and consistently underestimates ([Briand et al.](https://ehealthinformation.ca/web/default/files/wp-files/isern-97-22.pdf)); Chapman's estimator works with two, in simulation only ([El Emam & Laitenberger 2001](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/evaluating-capture-recapture-models-with-two-inspectors/812461632092372992-2431)); assumes the document does not change between samples | Unusable as a number; usable as a qualitative overlap signal |
| Author's judgement | The dominant stopping rule in written processes is a decider plus a quiet period: Rust's FCP closes after ten calendar days unless "substantial new arguments" arise ([rust-lang/rfcs](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs/blob/master/README.md)); W3C requires every issue "formally addressed" with rationale, and "a reviewer cannot block" ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/policies/process/)) | Supported as the decider, fed by the signals above |

The way these fit together is that **the author decides, and the agent's recommendation is built from severity and change-kind evidence**. Each kind of signal has a characteristic way of misleading. A count rewards nitpicking. A statistical estimate needs more independent reviewers than a single-author process runs, and it breaks once the text is edited between rounds. Fagan's 5% is the one quantitative trigger in the founding literature, and even there the moderator decides below the threshold. One Markov analysis of LLM self-correction adds a useful intuition. Iterating helps only while the error-correction rate divided by the error-introduction rate exceeds Acc/(1−Acc). At 90% correctness, fixes must outnumber regressions about nine to one, so **the bar for another round rises as the document improves** ([Liu & Meng, arXiv 2604.22273, via summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2604.22273)). That work is a preprint, it studies reasoning answers rather than documents, and it was read through a secondary summary.

On whether a second section review should be a standing rule or a recommendation, the evidence favours a **recommendation with stated triggers**, sitting on top of a standing obligation to verify fixes. No study validates any "N clean rounds" or "zero majors" stopping rule for document reviews. Calboreanu recommends "two consecutive full-scope passes return zero findings", but he did not use that rule himself and calls it case-bounded ([arXiv 2605.12280](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12280)). The round caps of 3 to 5 in community Claude Code tooling are budget guards that nobody has evaluated ([claudskills.com](https://claudskills.com/skills/code-quality-loop/SKILL.md)). By contrast, verifying applied rework by someone other than the author *is* a standing requirement in IEEE 1028 and Fagan. That part should stand. A full second section review is warranted when one of these holds: the last round produced accepted *material* findings; the fixes changed meaning rather than form (with doubt resolving to substantive, as at the W3C); the fixes touched a name or contract that other text consumes; or the rework was extensive. When none holds, a fresh full section review is expected to return the same stable core plus noise.

## Fresh LLM reviewers make repetition cheaper to run but noisier to read

Three things change when the reviewers are fresh Claude subagents and a human rules on every finding.

**First, independence is real but only partial.** Context-free reviewers are the N-fold ideal of independent samplers. But reviewers from the same model family share blind spots, so their findings overlap for reasons other than having exhausted the defects. If overlap between same-model runs is read capture–recapture style, it **overstates how close the document is to clean**. This is an inference from the correlated-failure caveat in the N-fold literature ([Kantorowitz](https://csaws.cs.technion.ac.il/~kantor/publications/nfold.pdf)) and from Calboreanu's warning that the same LLM family wrote and audited his specifications ([arXiv 2605.12280](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12280)). On a synthetic mini-specification, a four-vendor union caught all five seeded defects. That is too few defects to generalise from, but it points toward model diversity for recall ([arXiv 2605.12280](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12280)).

**Second, "no findings" is not a reachable target.** In CriticGPT, critiques led contractors to substantially lower the rating of flawless-rated completions **24% of the time, against 6% without the critique**. Longer critiques carried more nitpicks and hallucinated bugs ([McAleese et al., OpenAI](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00215v1)). The model's effort setting sets the noise floor. In Snyk's runs, Opus 4.6 Medium produced 5 unique extra findings, 60% of them stable. Sonnet 4.6 Medium produced 60, 61.7% of which appeared only once ([arXiv 2606.15762](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.15762)). Both are vendor studies on code. Taken together, they say that a round's raw finding count will **stop falling toward zero as the document improves**. The share that is nitpick or invented rises instead. The only stop signal robust to this is the absence of findings the author rules material. That fits the ledger-style criterion some practitioners use, "a full re-sweep surfaces zero findings not already in the ledger" ([claudskills.com](https://claudskills.com/skills/iterative-converging-audit/SKILL.md)). Neither has been evaluated.

**Third, the human ruling is the external check the self-correction literature says is essential, and it is also the binding cost.** Intrinsic self-correction degrades results, because models flip correct content to incorrect. Correction works when an external signal says what is wrong ([Huang et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.01798)). An author who rules on every finding supplies that signal. That should keep fix-induced error well below the rates seen in unsupervised loops, though no study has measured a human-gated document loop. The cost moves accordingly. Fagan's economics were about meeting time. LLM review runs are cheap, but the author's triage is not. At Beko, LLM review saw **73.8% of comments resolved, but mean PR closure time rose from 5 h 52 min to 8 h 20 min**, with complaints of unnecessary corrections and irrelevant comments ([Cihan et al., ICSE-SEIP 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18531)). The recommendation to run another round should therefore weigh the author's expected triage time, not the reviewer's compute.

Convergence can also go backwards. Calboreanu's per-round counts were **15, 8, 12, 2, 8, 1, 4, 1, 0**: a round of 2 was followed by a round of 8, attributed to cascading edits and a widening audit scope ([arXiv 2605.12280](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.12280)). A single quiet round after substantial fixes, or before the seam stage, is weak evidence that the work is finished.

## The evidence is old, code-centric, vendor-tinged and silent on this exact setting

The founding inspection evidence is **1976–2004**, from Fagan-style team inspections of code and of short requirements documents. The reinspection experiments used **31 student teams**, and the capture–recapture evaluations used 16–17-page specifications seeded with 27–29 known defects ([ISERN-97-22](https://ehealthinformation.ca/web/default/files/wp-files/isern-97-22.pdf)). Several key items were read only as abstracts or secondhand:

- IEEE 1028's wording comes from a mailing-list quotation.
- Gilb and Graham's exit numbers are secondary.
- The N-fold multipliers (1.5 times as many faults with two teams; 35% to 78% detection) are reported by later authors.

Every bad-fix rate is for code, and Jones's 7% has no published derivation. The change-ripple evidence for documents is indirect: clone rates (Juergens) and one LLM case study, Calboreanu's. That study has a single author, a single system, the same model family as author and auditor, and no single-pass control. Its journal venue is listed but was not independently confirmed. The LLM-reviewer evidence is almost all **2024–2026 preprints**. Snyk's stability study benchmarks against Snyk's own tool, and CriticGPT is OpenAI's study of its own critic. Several self-correction figures were read only through secondary summaries, and the CommonSenseQA numbers in Huang et al. conflict between them.

**Absent entirely:** any study of re-reviewing unchanged prose by fresh reviewers; any measured share of edit-induced inconsistencies that land in unchanged sections; any head-to-head comparison of delta, whole-document and seam review on specifications; any validated stopping rule for document reviews; any study of a review loop where a human rules on every finding; any guidance on how findings that are questions, rather than defects, should count toward exit; and any measurement of LLM cross-section contradiction detection at design-document length. ContraDoc's documents run to at most 2,200 tokens.

## Conclusion

The question "should the same review run twice?" turns out to be the wrong one. What a process should track is **which unchanged text a change has made stale**, and then send a *different* instrument at it: an anchored change-against-dependants check, or a seam review, not a rerun of the section review. That reframing keeps a no-repeat rule safe. It also makes the document's own shape a review cost. Every restated fact multiplies what an edit can contradict, so cross-referencing instead of restating, plus a mechanical search for the changed names and numbers, does more for re-review coverage than any extra round.

The process should therefore focus on four things, in this order. **Always verify applied fixes**, with a fresh reviewer whose prompt names the change and asks for its dependants. **Scope re-review by change kind**: classify each fix as editorial or substantive, settle doubt toward substantive, and send contract-touching changes to the seams. **Spend extra review on new lenses, not repeats.** And **let the author stop the loop** on the absence of new material findings, with the agent's recommendation resting on severity, change kind and recurrence across reviewers, never on a raw count or a capture–recapture number. Treat each of these as the best-supported reading of thin evidence, not as validated practice. The most useful thing this project could add to the record is its own measurement: how many accepted findings in each round were fix-induced or seam-exposed, against how many were found fresh.
